Profile

About

Timothy Xian-Ji Kuang is an Assistant Professor of Finance at National Changhua University of Education. His research focuses on macro-finance, factor investing, financial machine learning, financial engineering, and empirical asset pricing.

His work has appeared in Applied Economics, Applied Economics Letters, Computational Economics, and the International Review of Economics & Finance. He is the principal investigator of three research projects funded by Taiwan's National Science and Technology Council on VIX derivatives and machine learning.

His research received the 2025 EFMA Capital Markets Best Paper Award. He was also awarded the 2023 Doctoral Dissertation Fellowship in the Humanities and Social Sciences and a 2019 fellowship under the MOST Pilot Program for Cultivating Outstanding Doctoral Students.

Working Papers

ESG Rating Disagreement, Firm Valuation, and Accounting Profitability: Evidence from Taiwan

Tzu-Wen Wei, Tsai-Hsuan Yu, and Xian-Ji Kuang

Corresponding authorSSCINSTC Accounting A-

Abstract. This study examines whether disagreement among ESG rating providers affects firm valuation and accounting profitability in Taiwan’s retail-dominated equity market. Using disagreement measures from three rating sources for 918 firm-year observations over 2017–2023, we find that governance disagreement is robustly associated with lower Tobin’s Q, while environmental and social disagreement reduce return on assets. The negative effects are concentrated in the technology sector and firms with greater retail ownership. Our findings suggest that ESG rating inconsistency increases information uncertainty and leads to economically meaningful valuation discounts.

Submitted to Pacific-Basin Finance Journal

The Boundaries of Index Option Return Predictability: Evidence from Taiwan

Zong-Wei Yeh, Pin-Chi Chen, Xian-Ji Kuang, and Tzu-Chieh Lin

Corresponding authorSSCINSTC Finance A Tier 2

Abstract. Out-of-sample evidence on index option return predictability comes almost entirely from U.S. data. We provide, to our knowledge, the first systematic pseudo-real-time out-of-sample evaluation of monthly delta-hedged index-option return forecasts in an Asia-Pacific market, using recursive forecasts of dividend-inclusive returns on at-the-money Taiwan index (TXO) puts over 2002–2022; the mean put premium is about −0.71% per month. What predictability we find runs through the variance risk premium, and it is episodic rather than steady. Realized stock variance (SVAR), the state variable that tracks the premium’s physical leg, is the only one of 164 candidates whose full-sample slope survives the Holm correction; its out-of-sample R² of 11.03% over the 2007–2022 evaluation window (Clark–West p = 0.008) is earned mostly in crisis months and largely disappears after 2015. In a frozen 2023–2025 holdout the decay continues: the put premium shrinks to −0.24% per month and the SVAR forecast falls behind the historical mean. Point estimates concentrate the signal in puts, though formal put–call tests cannot separate the two sides, and the incremental value of predictive timing is economically negligible. Entry-date implied variance fits the target return largely by construction, so we analyze it separately as a channel variable rather than include it in the exogenous-predictor comparisons. Same-strike puts are persistently richer than calls—a wedge that closely tracks the index-futures basis, with a near-unit slope and an economically negligible mean gap: a market-wide carry phenomenon consistent with downside-insurance demand, though alternative carry-based explanations remain open.

Working paper

Good Jump and Bad Jump Risk Matters: Evidence from S&P500 Returns and Options

Shih-Kuei Lin, Hsing-Hua Chang, Hung-Wen Cheng, Xian-Ji Kuang, and Pai-Ta Shih

Presented at: EFMA 2025

Corresponding author2025 EFMA Capital Markets Best Paper Award

Abstract. In an affine GARCH setting, we extend an asymmetric double exponential jump-diffusion framework by modeling good and bad jump innovations with distinct exponential distributions and derive a closed-form option pricing solution. Including jump components considerably improves estimation, with the bad jump component dominating. Empirically, we extract good and bad variance risk premiums, both priced risk factors in cross-sectional tests. Time-series evidence reveals that the bad jump variance risk premium is particularly influential in forecasting returns. These findings highlight the critical interplay between expected returns, volatility, and asymmetric jump components in asset pricing.
